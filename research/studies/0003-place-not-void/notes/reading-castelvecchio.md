# Reading: Stavroulaki and Peponis on Castelvecchio

**Sources used.** I read all 14 pages of Stavroulaki & Peponis (page refs are the paper's own, 66.1–66.14). I also read Samia Rab, "Carlo Scarpa's Re-design of Castelvecchio," ACSA 86th Annual Meeting, pp. 443–451, in full. For Richard Murphy's book (Butterworth, 1990) I only had quotes relayed by Kimberly Tate's student essay (https://cargocollective.com/kimberlytate/In-the-Mood-for-Castelvecchio). I did not read the book itself, so treat those items as second-hand. The authors are Gianna (not Gina) Stavroulaki and John Peponis.

**Question, method, findings**
- **Question.** How the design makes spatial meaning. It is "a pedagogic device" aimed at "ways of seeing", not at narrative (66.1).
- **Method.** It covers only the sculpture galleries (five rooms) and the upper painting galleries. The authors hand-draw orientation and gaze diagrams (arrows per statue, gaze lines, intersections). They add an Omnivista isovist and projection-polygon analysis: flood-filled vantage points, mean depth, integration, at foot and eye level (66.7). It is explicitly "not tested against systematic data" on visitors (66.2).
- **Finding, sculpture rooms.** Statues form "a field of gazes". Movement is "an exploration of shifting relationships" (66.5).
- **Finding, painting rooms.** These are "intersecting optical frames of reference" (66.11).
- **Finding, overall.** Narrative is secondary. The meaning sits in embodied movement.

**Your specific questions**
- **Route order.** Five rooms are "axially connected into a single linear sequence" (66.3). Scarpa "moved the entrance towards the corner of the courtyard". It was originally mid-side, so visitors could go left or right. The result is a sequence that "allows no fundamental navigational alternatives" (66.12). Inside that line he adds "folding, undulating, oscillating, encircling" local movement (66.12). The painting galleries are walked back the other way.
- **Statue orientation.**
  - The paper does not say "no statue is seen head-on" in general. It says that at the threshold of the second room, visitors "have an overview of all the sculptures but do not see any of them frontally" (66.3, Fig. 2). Visitors are pushed to "walk around in order to face the front".
  - Of 19 statues, 12 face toward the main axis, 3 are parallel and 4 face away. Nine gaze at other statues. On 7 occasions two gazes intersect at a point that is "not otherwise occupied or marked" (66.3, 66.7). My inference is that facing the axis laterally means a visitor walking along it sees them side-on.
  - The paper's design logic is that gazes cross the circulation axis and pull visitors off it (66.7). It also says statue heights vary in section (66.3–66.4).
- **Thresholds and doors.**
  - In the sculpture rooms the doors are on the axis. In the painting rooms Scarpa "replaced the original transverse walls, whose openings were aligned along a central axis" with two lateral axes (66.8). One axis gives one-point perspective through successive doors. The other, along the river, is a skewed, elongated view.
  - A differently finished floor strip marks each path, and its "converging" edges make the depth visible (66.8).
  - Paintings sit near the edges of transverse walls. They are seen first from afar through the door, then at close range on leaving (66.10).
  - Easels face outward, so the visitor first meets their backs (66.9).
  - The paper does not discuss door width or compression. Its "thresholds" are perceptual: "continuous" versus "discontinuous" ones where things "come in or out of the field of vision" (66.5–66.6).
- **Paving joints.** "A detailed paving joint at each threshold" is not in this paper. The paper only says the floor "stops short of the old walls" as a "floating platform" with an irregular grid, forming a common frame for statues and visitors (66.11). Rab says that at one threshold, entrance room to sculpture gallery, Scarpa "details the paving edge… by a tiny cascade of levels formed from white Prun stone" (p. 448). That is one threshold, not every one. Tate's essay, citing Murphy, mentions a "variable reveal" between floor and wall.
- **Views ahead.** The paper does not describe withheld views as such. Its mechanisms are skewed rooms and statues set off-axis. A statue's face is revealed only from a particular spot. Large paintings are visible only from distance, and the easel backs interrupt the view (66.9).
- **Cangrande.** It stands at the far end of the Napoleonic wing in "a roofed but otherwise open space". It is visible from the courtyard and on crossings. Close up it is "almost always above and to the side". Just before the first painting room is "the only moment when the face of the knight is frontally seen, clearly smiling" (66.13). From a lower balcony it reads as "on a pedestal". Rab adds that Scarpa cut the roof back and "promoted multiple viewing points… from below, from above, and at close quarters" (p. 446–447). Tate adds that the statue is seen from behind and below on the way.

**Implications for the walkable field**
- **Orientation.** Angle objects to cross the route, not to face the camera. Aim some gazes at other objects and some to intersect at an empty spot, so the object field has relations among its own members. Give each object a position in section, not just plan. Face-on views should be earned by walking round.
- **Staging.** Keep one linear sequence, with local looping inside it. Show an overview of all objects at a room threshold, but no frontal view. Reserve one face-on moment (the Cangrande pattern) and repeat peripheral views on return.
- **Thresholds.** Aligned doors give a long view. Offsets and skews (the river axis) lengthen apparent depth. Mark a path with a different floor finish whose converging edges show depth. Place a work near a door edge so it is seen far on entry and near on exit.
- **Floor.** Use a floating plane that stops short of the walls, so floor and wall are visibly separate.

**Corrections to the earlier summary**
- "Moved the entrance so rooms read as one sequence": correct (66.12). It applies to the sculpture galleries, and the paper says the linear order is partly inherited and partly deliberate.
- "No statue faces you head-on": overstated. It holds for the second room as seen from its threshold. Even there, statues face the axis, and the Cangrande is explicitly seen frontally once.
- "Paving joint at each threshold": not supported here. Rab supports only one threshold. Murphy via Tate supports floor-to-wall reveals.
- "Compression then release": this paper does not support it. Its idea of a threshold is about visibility.
