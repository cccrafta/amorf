**User:**
what is exactly temporal experience of discovery? 

**Model:**
A **temporal experience of discovery** means treating the act of finding something not as an instantaneous retrieval of data, but as a process that unfolds over time—where duration, rhythm, and sequence actively shape how form and meaning are understood.

In standard web design, discovery is transactional and instant: you type a query, scroll a feed, or click a link, and the answer appears immediately in full view. By contrast, introducing a "temporal experience" shifts the interaction from fetching a static result to navigating an evolving encounter. 

It manifests across three key dimensions:

* **Spatial Unfolding (*Promenade* over Query):** Borrowing from architecture's *promenade architecturale*, the experience relies on sequential movement. Information isn't delivered all at once; it is revealed in stages as you navigate through thresholds, sightlines, and transitions. What you discover depends on where you came from and how long you stayed.
* **Dynamic Context and State:** The digital environment or object changes across time. Whether reacting to ambient conditions, user lingering, or systemic shifts, the artifact isn't static. What you observe at minute one differs from what is revealed at minute ten, giving digital content a sense of presence, atmospheric depth, or material patina.
* **Productive Friction:** Modern software optimizes for speed and zero latency. A temporal experience deliberately introduces pacing and cadence, encouraging slow perception. This directly mirrors the art of craft—where understanding and appreciating a material or structure requires lingering on subtle joinery, texture, and structural logic rather than consuming a finished visual at a glance.

In short, it moves the interaction away from *searching an index* and closer to *exploring an atmosphere*, where time itself becomes an active element of the design.

**Model:**
This completely grounds your earlier poetic description into a highly tangible, structural reality. It clarifies exactly what the "temporal experience" is: time isn't just passing; it is actively being recorded as geometry. 

By translating a chronological browsing history (the sequence of nodes) into a tangible, savable "space," you are turning the act of reading into an act of spatial creation. 

Here is a breakdown of why this concept works, along with the immediate UX and architectural challenges you'll need to solve.

### The Strengths of the Model

* **The *Promenade Architecturale* in UI:** Swapping the Cartesian Y-axis (vertical scrolling) for the Z-axis (depth/forward movement) fundamentally changes the user's relationship with the content. They are no longer an observer scanning a flat document; they are an inhabitant moving through a landscape. 
* **Time as a Tangible Artifact:** This is the most compelling part of your application. In standard browsers, your "history" is a flat, linear list of URLs. In *amorf*, the user's journey—the specific sequence of edges connecting the nodes—becomes a unique architectural footprint. A user's 20-minute session organically generates a custom "curated space" that can be saved, shared, or re-navigated.
* **Semantic Topology:** Calling a curated collection of nodes a "space" is conceptually tight. It treats knowledge not as a database, but as a territory. 

### The Friction Points (What to solve for)

* **Wayfinding and "The Void":** In traditional 2D web spaces, users always know where they are (top, middle, or bottom of a page). In 3D Z-axis navigation, users can easily get lost, disoriented, or stuck looking at empty space. You will need subtle visual anchors (a horizon line, a grid, atmospheric fog, or a breadcrumb trail) so they don't feel adrift in a digital vacuum.
* **Vestibular Accessibility (Motion Sickness):** Binding the scroll wheel to forward/backward movement and left/right rotation can be physically disorienting if the easing and camera acceleration aren't tuned perfectly. The movement must feel grounded and deliberate, not floaty or erratic.
* **The Initial Distribution Logic:** When a user enters a "space," how are the nodes placed on those coordinates? If Robbie House and Norwegian bricks are floating near each other, what defines that proximity? You will need a distinct curatorial logic for the X/Y/Z axes (e.g., X = time period, Z = material scale, Y = geographic region) so the space has structural meaning before the user even starts moving.

You are essentially building a spatial graph database where the querying mechanism is first-person traversal.

### The "About Page" / Manifesto Statement

### The Breakdown (How to explain the features architecturally)

When you need to explain *how* it works to a collaborator or a design jury, you can map your features directly to architectural concepts:

*   **The Entities (Nodes & Media) -> The Artifacts:** They are not just data points; they are rich, material artifacts (3D, sonic, visual) holding specific architectural or design knowledge.

*   **The Archive (2D browsing) -> The Index / The Catalog:** The grounded, structural foundation of the platform. It provides the necessary topological map when the user needs pure, unmediated access to the taxonomy.

This rhetoric positions *amorf* as an ongoing investigation into digital materiality and spatial curation, rather than just a novel piece of software.
